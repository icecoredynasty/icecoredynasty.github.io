Every IceCore franchise begins with the same resource.

**500 Coins.**

Nobody gets 501.

Nobody has to survive with 499.

Every General Manager begins with the same limit.

What those Coins become is entirely different.

## One Cap. Twenty Strategies.

Coins are IceCore's virtual salary currency.

They do not attempt to recreate NHL contracts.

They create a fantasy economy.

Every player occupying the active roster has a cost, and every General Manager has to decide where the franchise's resources belong.

Spend heavily on elite talent?

Build a balanced roster?

Search for undervalued players?

Bet on youth before the market catches up?

Protect an expensive core?

Create room for the next opportunity?

There is no correct answer.

There is only a **500 Coin limit**.

And twenty different ideas about how to use it.

## Price Is Not Value

The best player is not always the best asset.

A superstar at an enormous salary may produce more points.

A breakout player at a fraction of that cost may give his General Manager far more flexibility.

That distinction is where the IceCore economy begins.

**Salary tells you what a player costs.**

**Value tells you what that cost allows you to build around him.**

A 100-point player can be valuable.

So can the player producing 70 for a fraction of the salary.

The question is not only:

**How good is he?**

It is also:

**What can I build if I own him?**

## Every Coin Has an Opportunity Cost

Spending does not happen in isolation.

Every Coin committed to one player is a Coin that cannot be committed somewhere else.

An extra 30 Coins for a star may be worth it.

Or those same 30 Coins might become a defenseman, a goaltender upgrade or the flexibility to win another player later.

That is the tension.

IceCore does not ask General Managers to minimize salaries.

It asks them to decide where spending creates the most value for their franchise.

Sometimes the expensive player is absolutely worth it.

Sometimes walking away is the better move.

Knowing the difference is part of the game.

## The Market Sets the Price

The Salary Cap Draft creates the initial market.

General Managers compete against each other for players using the same finite resource.

Hype matters.

Reputation matters.

Age matters.

Production matters.

Need matters.

Timing matters.

And sometimes two managers simply refuse to stop bidding.

That is part of the market.

There is no universal price list telling twenty franchises what a player should cost.

The room decides.

Overpay today and the contract may become tomorrow's problem.

Find value and the same salary can become a competitive advantage.

## A Bargain Changes More Than One Position

Finding an underpriced player does more than improve one roster spot.

It creates options.

A cheap breakout may allow a franchise to spend aggressively elsewhere.

A productive young player may make an expensive veteran expendable.

Several efficient salaries may create enough flexibility to chase an elite player.

Value compounds across a roster.

That is why discovering a player before everybody else can matter long after the original acquisition.

The reward is not simply that the player scores.

It is what his salary allows the rest of the franchise to become.

## Breakouts Become Expensive

Cheap talent does not remain cheap forever.

Keeper salaries rise between seasons, with larger increases applied to higher-ranked players and additional protection against elite production remaining artificially inexpensive.

A player acquired before his breakout can still become an outstanding asset.

That discovery deserves to matter.

But success eventually costs money.

A bargain can survive.

It cannot remain frozen forever.

The system rewards discovery without allowing yesterday's breakout to become a permanent loophole in tomorrow's economy.

## Contracts Have Memory

A player's salary belongs to the asset.

Trade him and the salary travels with him.

A disappointing expensive player does not magically become cheap simply because another franchise acquires him.

A bargain does not become expensive merely because another GM wants him.

The contract remembers how the asset entered the market and what happened afterward.

That creates decisions familiar to franchise management.

Is the player worth the contract?

Can another organization use him better?

Does his salary fit a rebuilding franchise differently than a contender?

Do you accept the cost because the talent is difficult to replace?

Sometimes the most interesting part of a trade is not the player.

**It is the price attached to him.**

## Cap Space Is an Asset

Coins do not score fantasy points.

But having them available changes what a General Manager can do.

Cap space creates options.

It can create leverage in a draft.

It can make a trade possible.

It can allow a rebuilding franchise to attack the market while a contender is protecting an expensive core.

But unused Coins do not win championships either.

Flexibility only has value when a General Manager eventually turns it into something useful.

Spend everything too early and tomorrow's opportunity may be impossible.

Protect flexibility forever and today's opportunity may disappear.

The goal is not to have the most Coins.

The goal is to know when they are worth spending.

## Five Hundred

Every franchise receives the same number.

No franchise receives the same roster.

That is the point.

Coins create scarcity.

Scarcity creates choices.

Choices create different organizations.

And those choices follow the franchise from draft tables to keeper decisions, from trades to rebuilds, from one season into the next.

Five hundred is not a score to beat.

It is the boundary inside which a General Manager has to build.

**500 Coins. Spend them like they matter.**
